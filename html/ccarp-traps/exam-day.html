Exam day

You can know the material cold and still finish under the pass mark. Knowing the answers and scoring the points are separate skills. This page covers the second one.

Every item tells you how many responses to select. There is no official practice exam; the old one was retired in the move to Pearson VUE.

The clock: two passes

A suggested pace that finishes the first pass with time left for flagged items:

Item 21by 0:30
Item 42by 1:00
Item 63by 1:30, end of pass 1
30 minpass 2: flagged items only

  1. Pass 1: answer, or guess + flag + move on

    Answer everything you're sure of quickly. When an item makes you fight, put down your best guess, flag it, and keep going. A flag is a promise to come back, not permission to leave the item blank.

  2. Reach the last item with time on the clock

    Reaching every item beats polishing the ones you've already read.

  3. Pass 2: spend the remaining time on flags

    Hard thinking pays off here, once every item already has an answer.

The most expensive mistake in the room: spending nine minutes on one hard item while five easy ones at the end go unread.

Never leave a blank. A blank can't be marked correct, while a guess still has a chance. The official guide doesn't mention a guessing penalty.

Multiple-response items

The method

  1. Read the stem and find the constraint.
  2. Mark each option true or false against the stem, on its own.
  3. Select exactly the number asked. "Choose two" means two, not one and not three.

The trap

Hunting for the two options that "feel" right. Feelings go straight to the plausible distractors the exam is built from. A pick-two item has more wrong combinations than right ones, so judge each option separately.

Official scoring for multiple-response items (partial credit or all-or-nothing) isn't published. Assume every selection matters.

Reading traps

Skim-and-pick

You recognize the setup in the first line and pick the option that "obviously works", but the deciding clause ("reproducible for an auditor") sits three sentences later. Read to the end of the stem.

Topic, not constraint

You answer the topic ("this is about RAG") instead of the constraint ("…right after a document refresh"). The constraint decides the answer.

Best in the abstract

You pick the most capable design when the stem asks for the cheapest one that meets the SLA. "Best" is always relative to the stated constraint.

Preparation traps

Calibration

Practice that's easier than the real exam produces false confidence. In the course's opening story, a candidate cruised through practice and barely passed the real thing. Train above exam difficulty, on broken-system scenarios rather than definitions.

Allocation

Take a diagnostic in week 1. Study by weight in weeks 2–3 (D3, D1 and D4 before D2 and D7). In week 4, build one real system end to end and sit a timed 63-item mock.
